# Watering-hole excavation

Read this in Step 2 whenever the brief has an identifiable group of people. It is the one research
method that produces evidence desk research structurally cannot: what these people say to *each
other*, unprompted, in their own words.

The method is observational. You are not asking anyone anything — asking produces opinions, and
opinions about a future product are worthless. You are reading what was already said when nobody was
selling. (Amy Hoy's Sales Safari is the original; the discipline below is what makes it portable.)

## 1. Name the actor, then find where they talk unprompted

Be specific about who: not "small businesses" but "the person who does the invoicing at a 20-person
contractor." Then list the places that person talks without being asked:

- topic forums, subreddits, Discord and Slack communities, Facebook/LINE/WhatsApp groups
- product review sections and app-store reviews — especially the 2- and 3-star ones, where people
  explain what they needed and did not get
- support forums and issue trackers of the tools they already use, including adjacent tools
- Q&A sites, trade-association boards, professional bodies, industry-specific job posts
- comment sections on the publications they actually read, not the ones written about them

**Search in their language, not yours.** A venue where a community talks about the job in its own
jargon is worth ten articles written about that community, and it is usually invisible to
English-language, business-vocabulary queries. If the brief is tied to a place, run this in that
market's language and its native venues.

**No watering hole is itself a finding.** An audience with nowhere to talk to each other is expensive
to reach and slow to learn from — record it as a distribution risk rather than pushing on.

## 2. Record verbatim, never paraphrased

Paraphrasing is the failure. The moment a complaint is rewritten in category language ("users
experience friction in the reconciliation workflow"), the specific thing that made it a business
disappears and the portfolio starts sounding like every other portfolio. Keep the words.

For each observation worth keeping:

```
"[exact quote]" — [who they appear to be / context] — [link, date]
Job: what they were trying to get done
Today: what they use or do instead, and what it costs them
Tried and abandoned: what they already gave up on, and why
Their words for it: the nouns and verbs they use, not ours
```

Never invent or compose a quote. Never write "many people say" — count the instances you actually
found and say the number. If a claim rests on one thread, say one thread.

## 3. What to listen for

- **"I ended up just…"** — the workaround. This is the substitute, and it is usually free.
- **"finally", "at last"** — relief; something was painful for a long time.
- **"instead of", "rather than"** — a switch already happened; the forces behind it are visible.
- **"does anyone know how to…"** asked repeatedly by different people — an unmet job. Someone
  answering that same question over and over is doing work a business could do.
- **"we gave up on…"** — a failed prior attempt with the reason attached, which is the hardest thing
  to get from search results.
- **arguments about which tool to use** — the real competitive set, in the buyer's own framing.
- **prices mentioned in passing** — what they already pay, which no pricing page will tell you.

## 4. What it feeds downstream

- **Signal cards** — quotes are `Observed`, at the strongest evidence quality this skill can get
  without talking to anyone.
- **The substitute test (gate zero)** — this is where the free, informal, manual, family, community,
  or spreadsheet version becomes visible. It rarely appears anywhere else.
- **The incumbent test** — complaints about a named product tell you which segment it fails and why,
  which is exactly what the collision pass has to answer.
- **The plain-language scene** — the concept can be written in the buyer's own vocabulary instead of
  the mechanism vocabulary that makes portfolios unreadable.

## 5. State the coverage honestly

The original method budgets tens of hours of human reading. Reading faster is not the same as reading
deeply, so say what was actually covered: how many venues, roughly how many threads, over what date
range, in which languages. A single subreddit skimmed for twenty minutes is a lead, not an evidence
base, and should be labelled that way.

Name the blind spot too. Forums over-represent the vocal, the online, and the already-technical; the
people who never adopted anything, never complained in public, or do not use the internet in this
part of their life leave no trace here. That silence is not absence of demand — and it is not
evidence of demand either.
