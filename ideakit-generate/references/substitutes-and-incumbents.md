# Substitutes and incumbents — the test this skill kept failing

Read this in Step 5 for every finalist, before any gate. Two errors recur often enough to have
killed whole runs of this skill, and they are opposites of each other.

## Error 1 — treating an existing company as a wall

"Someone already does this" is not a reason to stop. It is the cheapest demand evidence available.
The empty market is usually the worse one: nobody has proven anyone pays, so the founder pays to
find out. For a solo founder with no audience, that is the most expensive purchase available.

Ask instead: **who does the incumbent structurally fail, why can't they fix it, and can we take
those customers?** Then name the ONE dimension on which the new venture is 10× better and the
structural reason the incumbent cannot follow — cannibalisation, channel conflict, a cost floor made
of salaries, a licence, a liability, a parent company that owns a competing brand, an ad policy, a
banking posture. "They are slow" or "they have bad UX" is not a structural reason.

A dead competitor is the most useful find of all. Establish what actually killed it — burn rate,
customer-acquisition cost, a licence, an unprofitable operational leg — because that is nearly
always a verdict on a *business shape*, not on the demand.

## Error 2 — pricing against a market that already clears for free

This is the error that kills more finalists than any competitor does, and desk research is
structurally blind to it: free and informal substitutes leave almost no searchable trace. Language
models are also measurably worse than a plain web search at epistemic diversity, and their coverage
of non-English, local, offline, and informal activity is the weakest part of their distribution
(arXiv:2510.04226). The competitor you cannot find is disproportionately likely to be the one that
kills you.

So for every finalist, answer explicitly, in the artifact:

> **Substitute test.** What do these people do today, at what price? Name the free, informal,
> manual, family, community, volunteer, state-provided, spreadsheet, group-chat, or
> already-part-of-someone's-job version. What does it cost them in money, time, and dignity? What
> does our version add that is worth *more than the gap* between the two prices?

Concrete forms this takes, from real runs:

- a relative, neighbour, or friend already does the errand for petrol money;
- a state or volunteer service already does it for nothing (village health volunteers, a municipal
  programme, a public-health scheme, a university's free public map);
- an industry body, consultancy, or vendor gives the analysis away as marketing;
- the workflow already runs on a group chat and a spreadsheet, and the participants do not
  experience that as a problem;
- an adjacent product the buyer already pays for covers it as a secondary feature;
- a licensed incumbent sells the same protection at a price no unlicensed newcomer can undercut.

Two rules follow:

1. **Free plus tolerable beats paid plus better, almost always.** Displacing a free substitute needs
   a step change, not an improvement — usually because the free version is unavailable at the moment
   of need, is socially costly to ask for, or cannot produce something the payer needs (a record, a
   guarantee, a signature, reach the payer does not have).
2. **Price against the payer's world, not the user's.** The strongest escape from a free substitute
   is usually a different payer whose money is worth different amounts — an overseas wage, an
   employer's replacement cost, an importer's tariff exposure — rather than a better product.

## Failure modes to name in the artifact

- Concluding "no competitor found" from search results. That is a retrieval artifact, and it is
  most misleading exactly in the niche, local, and non-English markets where opportunity is sought.
- Reporting a market size or competitor count from model priors. Fabrication rates scale with the
  obscurity of the topic — the more underserved the niche, the more the model invents.
- Treating a funded incumbent's existence as a verdict, or a dead one's failure as proof of no
  demand, without establishing which part of the business actually broke.
